## Structure of a good narrative
A grounds-of-suspicion narrative must let a reader who has never seen the case understand it. Cover:
WHO - the subjects, their accounts, roles in the scheme, profile (occupation, declared income, onboarding channel, account age).
WHAT - the activity: channels, number and value of transactions, counterparties.
WHEN - the period, first and last suspicious activity, and dwell time between credit and debit.
WHERE - locations of branches, ATMs and counterparties, and the states of complainants.
WHY - the specific red flags and why the activity is inconsistent with the customer profile.
HOW - how funds moved through the network, from source to cash-out or onward destination.

## Style rules
Use factual, neutral language. State amounts in INR with Indian digit grouping. Separate facts from inferences ("analysis indicates", "appears to"). Do not speculate about guilt. Reference supporting documents (complaints, KYC notes, device logs) and keep each ground of suspicion linked to evidence.

## Grounds of suspicion
List each ground as: indicator - evidence - reference to the applicable policy clause. Typical grounds for mule networks are rapid pass-through, income mismatch, fan-in from unrelated remitters, device sharing, cyber-fraud complaints and cash-out via ATM or crypto exchanges.

## Action taken
Record actions such as debit freeze or lien marking under SOP-02, enhanced due diligence, re-KYC requests, closure decisions, and coordination with law-enforcement agencies. State whether the customer has been contacted, without tipping off.
